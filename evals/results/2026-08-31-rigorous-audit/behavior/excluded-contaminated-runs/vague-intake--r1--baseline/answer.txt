The best approach is to **not** start by building a broad “study better” platform. That category is too vague, and most products in it collapse into generic notes, task lists, or AI chat.

Start with a narrow wedge:

1. Pick one student segment.
   Example: first-year engineering students, pre-med students, law students, students preparing for finals in content-heavy courses.

2. Pick one painful study job.
   Example: turning lecture slides into practice questions, building a revision plan before exams, tracking weak topics across courses.

3. Solve that one job better than existing tools.
   The strongest wedge is usually:
   **course material in -> active recall + spaced review plan out**
   That is closer to real learning outcomes than “AI tutor” or “note workspace”.

4. Validate before building much.
   Talk to 15-20 students.
   Ask for their actual workflow, last exam prep process, tools used, where they waste time, and what they abandon.
   If possible, manually do the service for a few of them before automating it.

5. Define one hard metric.
   Good examples:
   `weekly active study sessions`
   `cards/questions completed per week`
   `days retained in revision plan`
   `self-reported exam confidence improvement`
   Avoid vanity metrics like signups or chats started.

A strong first MVP would be:
- Upload notes/slides/syllabus
- Auto-generate practice questions and summaries
- Build a study schedule around exam dates
- Track mastery by topic
- Remind students to review weak areas

That is much better than launching with forums, social features, broad course marketplaces, or a generic chatbot.

The main risk is building something students try once but do not return to. So design for a repeated loop:
`import material -> study -> get tested -> see weakness -> come back tomorrow`
